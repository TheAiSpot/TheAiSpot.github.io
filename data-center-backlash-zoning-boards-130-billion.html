The AI Backlash Moved to Your County Zoning Board. In Three Months It Stalled $130 Billion.

Staff Reporter• August 10, 2026• TheAiSpot

Last week the supervisors of Bedford Township, Pennsylvania, unanimously passed a twenty-page ordinance regulating data centers. Setbacks. Lighting. Noise limits. Environmental impact. Electricity use.

No company has approached Bedford Township about building a data center. There is no application, no rezoning request, no developer at the podium. The township wrote twenty pages of rules against a thing that has not happened to it yet.

That is the story of the AI infrastructure buildout in August 2026, and it is not being written in Congress. It is being written by township supervisors, county commissioners, and planning commission members who have watched what happened somewhere else and decided to get ahead of it.

The Number: $130 Billion in One Quarter

Data Center Watch, which has tracked organized local resistance since 2023, counted at least 75 data center projects worth roughly $130 billion blocked or delayed between January and March of 2026. That is the largest three-month total the group has ever recorded — and it roughly equals the damage tallied across all of 2025.

The supporting numbers matter more than the headline figure, because they describe a movement rather than a bad quarter. Active opposition groups more than doubled after the end of 2025, to 833, and now span 49 states. Petition signatures gathered in that single quarter nearly matched every signature collected in the entire second half of 2025.

A word on the source, since the number is doing a lot of work. Data Center Watch is a research project of 10a Labs, a firm led by former Columbia adjunct professor Robert McKenzie that sells intelligence and investigative research to large technology companies. The tracking project is run by analyst Miquel Vila, who monitors local news, government meeting records, and community social media groups, using a mix of large language models and human analysts on open-source material. The group says the project is deliberately kept separate from 10a Labs’ client work and is not client-funded. It is, in other words, an industry-adjacent firm counting the industry’s losses — which cuts against the count being inflated, not for it.

In the first six weeks of 2026, more than 300 data center bills were filed in 30 state legislatures. Maine came within a single House vote of becoming the first state in American history to ban data centers outright.

These are the ones worth noticing. A community fighting a specific developer is a land-use dispute. A community writing rules against a developer who has never called is something else — it means the reputation of the asset class now arrives before the asset does.

The Numbers Nobody Can Agree On

Palm Beach County is the clearest illustration of why these fights get so hot. Commissioners rejected the $2 billion Project Tango near Loxahatchee on a 5–1 vote in July, and the deciding issue was water. The developer estimated the facility would use 5,000 gallons a day. County staff put the figure at 100,000 gallons a day — twenty times higher.

That gap is the whole problem in miniature. A county commissioner is being asked to approve an industrial facility whose resource consumption is disputed by a factor of twenty, on a timeline set by the applicant, with an economic case supplied by the applicant. In Plymouth Township, Pennsylvania, the developer of a Conshohocken Road site released a study projecting $424 million in local tax revenue over 20 years just ahead of an August 6 zoning hearing. In Cumberland County, Pennsylvania, disclosed records showed a developer had spent nearly two years in private discussions with Monroe Township officials before the proposal was announced publicly in April.

None of that is illegal. All of it explains why a room full of residents shows up angry.

Why Your Power Bill Is In This Story

The zoning fights would stay local if not for electricity, which is regional and shows up on everyone’s bill.

PJM Interconnection, the grid operator covering thirteen states from Illinois to New Jersey, ran a capacity auction that cleared at its price ceiling of $329.17 per megawatt-day, up 22% from $269.92 the previous year. Consumers across PJM will pay a total of $16.1 billion for capacity in the twelve months beginning June 2026. In the prior auction, data centers were responsible for 63% of the price increase — about $9.3 billion recovered from customers in higher rates.

PJM’s forecast peak load for the 2026/2027 delivery year rose by more than 5,400 megawatts in a single year, driven largely by data center expansion. Nationally, data centers consumed over 4% of US electricity in 2022 and are projected to reach roughly 9% by 2030.

This is what converts a zoning dispute into a political movement. A resident of Ohio or Maryland who has never seen a data center still gets the bill for one.

What a Local Moratorium Actually Does
A moratorium pauses permits inside one jurisdiction for a fixed window — typically six to eighteen months — while staff draft permanent rules. It does not cancel a project, and it does not reduce anyone’s electricity rate. Prices are set in rate cases at state public utility commissions, proceedings most residents never attend. Local boards control where; regulators control who pays. Winning one has never automatically won the other.

The Part That Should Worry the Industry

That last distinction was, until recently, the industry’s reliable fallback: lose a county, win the state. It is eroding.

More than 300 data center bills were introduced across 30 state legislatures in the first six weeks of 2026, marking a decisive shift from courting the industry with incentives toward regulating it. At least 18 states have taken up bills creating separate rate classes for very large electricity users. As of May 2026, 23 states had approved at least one large-load tariff, with seven more pending — mechanisms designed to make data centers pay for the grid capacity they require instead of spreading it across residential bills.

Several are already law. Oregon’s POWER Act, signed in August 2025, directs the state utility commission to create a separate rate category for large loads. South Dakota’s SB 135 was signed in March 2026. Oklahoma’s Data Center Consumer Ratepayer Protection Act, HB 2992, was signed in May 2026 and requires electric suppliers to write separate terms and tariffs for large-load customers.

The more aggressive proposals go further. Ohio House Bill 983, introduced by Rep. Jennifer Gross, would require a local citizen vote before any new data center, set environmental standards, and end tax abatements — applying retroactively to more than 150 existing facilities. Moratorium proposals have been introduced in 14 states, by legislators from both parties.

Which is the detail that makes this durable. In districts hosting large data center projects, roughly 55% of Republicans and 45% of Democrats who took a public position took it against the project. There is no partisan coalition to split here, and no national campaign to outspend — just several hundred separate local proceedings, each with its own record, each requiring its own consent.

What to Take From This

  • The binding constraint on AI capacity is no longer chips or capital. It is permission. Money and silicon can be procured in months; a county that has decided against you takes years, and there is no premium price that clears the market.
  • Pre-emptive ordinances are the signal to watch. Bedford Township, Yakima County, and Smithville all acted with no project on the table. When jurisdictions regulate in advance, the industry has lost the default — every site now starts from a negative presumption.
  • Contested numbers cost more than bad numbers. A twentyfold disagreement over water use killed a $2 billion project in Palm Beach County. Developers who arrive with figures a county’s own staff will not corroborate are handing opponents their best argument.
  • Rate design is the real battlefield now. Twenty-three states with approved large-load tariffs is a structural change, not a protest. It determines whether AI infrastructure pays its own grid costs or socializes them — and unlike a zoning vote, it applies statewide and permanently.
  • Tax abatements are becoming a liability. A 50% break for 15 years reads differently to residents whose electricity bills are rising. Ohio’s retroactive proposal shows what happens when that arithmetic becomes a campaign issue.

The Takeaway

The AI industry spent 2025 and 2026 arguing about frontier model rules, export controls, and safety frameworks — the fights that happen in Washington, where there are lobbyists and a small number of decision-makers who can be reached. Meanwhile the thing that actually constrains AI capacity turned out to be a Tuesday evening meeting in a county building where roughly 80% of the speakers are opposed and the commission votes anyway, and then gets appealed.

$130 billion in one quarter is not a protest movement. It is a permitting failure at industrial scale, and it is compounding: opposition groups doubled in three months and now exist in 49 states, which means the playbook is spreading faster than any individual project can be approved.

The industry’s answer so far has been the one it used for the last decade — jobs numbers, tax revenue projections, and abatements. Bedford Township wrote its ordinance before anyone got the chance to make that pitch. That is the part worth sitting with: in a growing number of places, the argument is being lost before it is offered.
