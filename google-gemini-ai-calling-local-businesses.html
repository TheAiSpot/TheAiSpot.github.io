Google Just Shipped a Phone That Calls Stores for You. Nobody Asked the Stores.

Staff Reporter• August 12, 2026• TheAiSpot

Buried in a keynote otherwise devoted to camera bars and a $29 Bluetooth tracker, Google described the Pixel 11’s task automation in one short sentence: Gemini “can also dial businesses to complete tasks.”

That is the whole announcement. It is also the most consequential thing said on stage in New York today, and it is the part that will not appear in a single hands-on review, because you cannot photograph it.

The rest of Made by Google 2026 was a normal hardware refresh. The Pixel 11 starts at $899, a hundred dollars up from last year, with 256GB as the new baseline. The Pixel 11 Pro Fold lands at $1,899 in October. The Pixel Tag, Google’s long-delayed answer to the AirTag, is $29 and arrives 11 November. The Tensor G6 is the first major Android chip built on TSMC’s 2nm process, and Google claims Gemini Nano 4 runs on-device tasks up to 3.5× faster using 3.5× less energy, on a TPU with 50% more compute and double the memory bandwidth.

All of which is table stakes. The dialing is not.

What Is Actually New, and What Was Already There

Gemini dialing businesses from a handset is not a feature invented this morning. It is the last mile of a stack Google has been assembling for roughly three years, and it is worth separating what shipped today from what it plugs into — because the parts have been arriving quietly enough that most people missed them.

On the Search side there is “Let Google Call.” Search for a product with “near me” and a button appears. You specify the item, a price range, brands, whatever else matters. Google’s voice agent — Duplex, now driven by Gemini — then telephones nearby stores, asks about stock, pricing and promotions, and sends you a summary by text or email. It launched for electronics, toys, and health and beauty.

On the transaction side there is agentic checkout. You tell Google to track a product and buy it if the price drops inside a budget you set. When it does, Google surfaces the purchase, confirms shipping and payment, and — with your explicit approval — completes the transaction through Google Pay. It is live with Wayfair, Chewy, Quince and a set of Shopify merchants in the US.

Underneath both sits the standard nobody outside retail noticed. In January, at the National Retail Federation conference, Google announced the Universal Commerce Protocol, built with Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair, Target and Walmart, and interoperable with the Agent Payments Protocol, Agent2Agent and MCP. Its purpose is to let an agent handle discovery, checkout and post-purchase support without a bespoke integration per retailer.

This kind of serendipity is where the best of commerce happens. — Tobi Lütke, CEO, Shopify, on agent-mediated shopping

Read that list of participants again. Shopify, Etsy, Wayfair, Target, Walmart. Every one of them signed a protocol. Lowe’s, Michaels, Poshmark and Reebok run Google’s Business Agent inside Search. These are consenting parties who negotiated terms.

The independent hardware store that has the part you need is not on that list. It will find out it is participating when the phone rings.

The Rules Google Wrote for the Other End of the Call

To Google’s credit, the receiving end is not undocumented. There is a Business Profile help page covering automated calls placed on behalf of customers, and it is more specific than most people would expect.

The calls are free to both sides. The AI identifies itself as an automated caller. Google says it “limits the number of calls for the same info” and avoids late-night and early-morning hours. Triggers are bounded: appointment bookings, restaurant wait times, price and availability confirmation, business hours, and in-demand inventory checks. Stores can opt out.

And then there is the line that changes the texture of the whole thing.

These calls are monitored and recorded for quality assurance. — Google Business Profile Help, “About automated calls from Google on behalf of customers”

A machine telephones a small business, on behalf of a customer the business has never met, to extract commercially useful information, and the exchange is recorded. Every element of that is disclosed. None of it was agreed to in advance.

Opting Out Is Four Different Procedures

To stop the callsWhat it takes
During a callSay “Please remove my business from your list” or “Please stop calling my business.” Google says it honours this going forward
In settingsBusiness Profile → More → Business Profile settings → Advanced settings → toggle “Google automated calls and text messages.” Requires a verified business
Phone-tree mapping callsA separate opt-out. Call +1-650-206-5555 and leave a voicemail with your business name, number and request
Texts and WhatsAppReply with the language-specific stop word
Excluded statesNo action needed. Google does not place these calls in 5 US states

Four mechanisms, one of which requires you to already know that a distinct category of call exists, and to opt out of it by voicemail. The settings route requires a verified Business Profile — which many small operators have never claimed, because until recently the cost of not claiming it was showing up with slightly stale hours.

Note also what is not on that list: a way to find out how many times you were called before you noticed.

Five States Where Google Will Not Do This

The feature is live in every US state except Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Montana and Nebraska, plus select international regions.

Google does not explain the exclusions, and the sensible thing is not to invent a reason. What can be said factually is that all five carry their own automated-calling statutes rather than relying on federal rules alone. Indiana’s Automated Dialing Machine Act — Indiana Code 24-5-14 — requires prior express consent from the resident being called and carries penalties reaching $25,000 per call; it has survived constitutional challenge. Montana bars automated calls under MCA 45-8-216. Nebraska’s autodialer provision sits at Neb. Rev. Stat. § 86-236, Louisiana’s at La. Stat. § 45:810, and Minnesota’s restrictions on automated dialing and announcing devices are broad enough to have caught political campaigns.

Draw the obvious inference or don’t. Either way, the map tells you something the product page does not: the parts of America where an AI agent may not call a business on your behalf are the parts that legislated about robots on the phone before anyone thought to build this.

The Asymmetry
When a human customer calls and gets a full voicemail box, the business loses one sale and usually never learns about it. When an agent calls and gets a full voicemail box, it moves to the next result in milliseconds and reports back that the item was found elsewhere. The lead does not bounce — it silently reroutes to a competitor who answered. The business does not get a missed call. It gets nothing, and no signal that anything happened at all.

The Perverse Incentive Nobody Designed

Follow the opt-out to its conclusion. An employee at a busy shop picks up, hears a synthetic voice, and says what a lot of people say: we don’t talk to robots, take us off your list.

Google honours it. That is the documented behaviour and it is the correct behaviour. But the practical effect, as practitioners in local search have begun pointing out, is that the business has now removed itself from the mechanism by which a growing share of “is this in stock near me” questions get answered. It did not decline an advertisement. It declined to be an answer.

Google has not published data on how often that happens, how many businesses have opted out, or what share of “near me” retail queries now resolve through a call rather than an inventory feed. Those numbers exist somewhere. They are the numbers that would let a shop owner make an informed decision, and they are the ones nobody outside Google can see — the same structural gap that showed up in the publisher opt-out Google shipped last week, where the switch arrived and the data needed to price it did not.

The pattern is consistent enough to name. Google builds an agentic surface, defaults it on, provides a genuine opt-out, and withholds the measurement that would tell the counterparty whether using it costs them anything.

What to Take From This

  • The agent left the browser. Every previous version of this argument was about scraping, crawling and screen-reading — activity confined to Google’s own infrastructure. A phone call is a real-world action with a person on the other end who has to stop what they are doing. That is a different category of thing, and it shipped on a consumer handset today.
  • Consent is now structurally lopsided. The buyer opted in by tapping a button. The merchant platforms opted in by signing a protocol. The local business is the only participant whose involvement is presumed, and the only one who has to take action to escape it.
  • Not answering an AI well is becoming a business risk. Phone trees, hold music, voicemail and staff who hang up on synthetic voices were all rational until this year. Each is now a way to lose customers you will never know existed.
  • Five states are running the control experiment. Indiana, Louisiana, Minnesota, Montana and Nebraska will spend the next year as the only places where local commerce is not agent-mediated by default. Whether their businesses do better or worse is an answerable question.
  • Watch the categories expand. It started with electronics, toys, health and beauty. Restaurant wait times and appointment bookings are already in the documentation. There is no technical reason the list stops there.

The Takeaway

Calling four hardware stores to ask whether a part is in stock is a genuinely tedious errand, and handing it to software is a genuinely good idea. That is not in dispute, and the version Google shipped is more careful than it had to be: the AI says what it is, the calls are rate-limited, nobody gets rung at 3am, and there are four separate ways to make it stop.

What is missing is the step where anyone asked. The customer consented. Walmart, Target, Shopify, Etsy and Wayfair consented in writing. The shop with three employees and one phone line was enrolled by default into a system that records its calls, extracts its pricing, and quietly routes the sale elsewhere if it takes too long to pick up.

Opt-out is a defensible design when the cost of participating is a mildly annoying email. It is a different proposition when participating means answering the phone correctly or disappearing from the answer. Google has built the most useful version of this that anyone has built. It has also, for the second time in a week, shipped a switch to the party with the least information about whether flipping it will hurt.
